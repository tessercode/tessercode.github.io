TEMPO: A Stochastic Benchmarking Protocol for Evaluating Temporal Robustness in Spiking Neural Networks

July 2026

Spiking neural networks are set apart from conventional architectures by their leaky-integrator dynamics and temporal memory, yet the benchmarks most often used to evaluate them don't exercise those properties. In datasets such as N-MNIST and DVS-Gesture, the discriminative information lies in spatial features, so collapsing all of the spike timing into a single bin leaves classification accuracy largely intact. Like my Morse Spike Encoder project, TEMPO turns to Morse code, where meaning lives entirely in the duration of the marks and the silences between them. Existing Morse benchmarks assume machine-perfect timing, though, and real human operators are anything but.

TEMPO (Temporal Encoding Morse Protocol for Operators) places human operator variability at the center of its design. Three stochastic parameters, the dash-to-dot ratio, word-level speed, and per-element jitter, model an operator's characteristic "fist," with distributions fitted to 44 real recordings. A proof-of-concept multi-timescale network reaches 75.7% accuracy, and shuffling spike order while preserving counts collapses that to a count-based baseline, confirming the task can't be solved without timing. The paper was published in Neuromorphic Computing and Engineering as open access, and the repository includes the encoding library, pre-built datasets, and the notebooks for every experiment in the paper, with 75.7% standing as an open baseline for future architectures.

Software Simulator Survey

2026

As I was starting my journey in Neuromorphic Computing, I wanted to get a better understanding of the landscape of software simulators for spiking neural networks. It was difficult for me as a newcomer as I was reading various papers to understand which simulators were open source and publicly available and those that were not. Some were software frameworks or libraries that only supported hardware simulators and others simply provided tools to extend the capabilities of existing software simulators, but did not provide the simulator itself. Some simulators are released open source, but have not been maintained since that initial release while others provide extensive documentation and regular updates.

What began as a simple literature review in September 2024 quickly turned into a months-long project to identify the current state of open-source software simulators for spiking neural networks, cataloging supported neuron models, learning mechanisms, and supported hardware platforms. It also builds upon my previous work with software engineering research and the open-source CHAOSS project to develop a viability scorecard that I use to examine the code freshness, documentation, and community engagement of each simulator. The resulting paper, Finding Order in CHAOSS: A Survey of Open-Source Spiking Neural Network Software Simulators, was published in IEEE Access in 2026. The open-source repository includes the simulator data, research notes, methodology, and worksheet for others to use and build upon in their own research journeys.
